The short version

Card images and the text read from them never leave your device. There is no account, no sign-up, no analytics and no server. Lanyard makes no network requests of any kind. The only data that moves anywhere is what you export yourself, or what you save into your own Contacts — and both of those stay on your device unless you send them somewhere.

What Lanyard collects

Nothing. In App Store Connect terms the app's data collection is declared as Data Not Collected, and that declaration is accurate rather than aspirational: the app contains no analytics framework, no advertising identifier, no crash reporter and no third-party SDK of any kind. It has no network code, so there is no setting that could turn collection on and no future configuration change that could start it without a new version of the app.

What stays on your device

Everything Lanyard holds:

  • the photograph of each card, stored at a size big enough to read and no bigger;
  • the text the recogniser read from it, kept verbatim so you can check any field against it;
  • the parsed contact — name, job title, company, phone numbers, email addresses, postal address, websites — and any notes you add;
  • the events you create and which cards were scanned at each;
  • your settings.

All of it lives in one file inside the app's own container, with the card images beside it, plus a small summary in a shared App Group container so the widgets can show counts. iOS keeps both private to Lanyard. This data is included in an encrypted iPhone or iCloud backup if you make one; that backup is between you and Apple, and the developer has no access to it.

Turning off Keep the card image in Settings means the photograph is never written to disk at all.

How recognition works

Lanyard uses Apple's Vision framework, which performs text recognition on the device's own hardware. No part of the image is sent to Apple, to the developer, or to any other service. Parsing — deciding which line is the name, which number is the mobile, which country a postcode belongs to — happens afterwards, also on the device, in code that has no network access.

This is a genuine technical property of the app rather than a policy commitment: there is nowhere for a card to go.

The person whose card you scanned

A business card is somebody else's personal data. In the UK, the EU and a growing number of other jurisdictions, scanning one into an app you control makes you the controller of that data — particularly if you scan cards for work.

Lanyard is designed so that this responsibility stays contained and easy to discharge. The developer never receives, stores or processes the card, and is not a controller or a processor for it: there is no transfer to be documented, no sub-processor to disclose and no international transfer to assess. If the person on a card asks you to delete it, deleting the card in Lanyard really deletes it — the record and the image — with no server-side copy anywhere for either of us to chase.

What you do with a card after exporting it is between you and them. If you put it into a CRM, send a marketing email or add it to a mailing list, the obligations that attach to that are yours, and the rules on unsolicited business-to-business email differ considerably between countries. It is worth knowing which ones apply to you.

Camera, photos and contacts

Camera. Used to photograph a card, and only while the scanner is open. Lanyard does not record video, does not run the camera in the background, and does not keep a frame you did not capture.

Photo library. Requested only if you choose to scan a card from a photo you already took. iOS shows you the picker; Lanyard receives only the images you select and never has access to the rest of your library.

Contacts. Requested only to add a contact, and on iOS 18 and later Lanyard asks for write-only access specifically. It never reads your existing contacts. Duplicate detection compares scans against each other inside Lanyard, which is why the app does not need to see your address book to do it.

Declining any of these leaves the rest of the app working normally.

What Apple sees

Purchases. If you subscribe to the Full File, Apple processes the payment. The app receives only a signed receipt saying whether a subscription is active — never your name, your email or your payment details. Apple's handling of that transaction is covered by Apple's privacy policy.

App Store analytics. Apple reports aggregate, anonymised figures to developers, such as how many devices installed the app in a country. These cannot identify you and the app plays no part in producing them.

Your rights, and theirs

Data protection law — including the UK and EU GDPR, and comparable laws elsewhere — gives people rights of access, correction, erasure, portability and objection over personal data held about them. Those rights are exercised against whoever holds the data.

The developer holds none, so there is nothing to disclose, correct or delete on request: no card, no image, no contact record, no account, no log. You have complete and direct control — edit any field in the app, export everything as a CSV or vCard, and erase it all by deleting a card, using Settings › Delete everything, or deleting the app.

If somebody whose card you scanned exercises their rights against you, the app gives you what you need: their record is findable by search, exportable, and deletable in a way that removes both the parsed data and the original image.

Keeping and deleting data

Your cards are kept for as long as you keep the app installed, and no longer. There is no automatic expiry, because a contact from three years ago is often exactly the one you want. Deleting the app removes its container and everything in it, including every card image. There is no server-side copy to request the deletion of, and no way to recover a deleted file — so export first if you want to keep the record.

Security

Your data is protected by iOS's own file protection, which encrypts app data at rest while the device is locked. Setting a passcode, and turning on Face ID or Touch ID, is what makes that protection meaningful; an unlocked phone with no passcode protects nothing. Given that this app holds other people's contact details, that is worth doing.

Because no data is transmitted, there is no network channel to intercept and no server to breach. That is not a claim about how carefully the servers are run — it is a claim that they do not exist.

Children

Lanyard is a tool for professional use and is not directed at children. It collects nothing from anybody, of any age.

Changes

If this policy changes, the date at the top changes with it, and the previous wording stays in the public history of the repository that hosts this page. A change that materially affected how data is handled — in particular, anything that introduced a network request — would be announced in the app before it took effect.
